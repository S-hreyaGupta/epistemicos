This study bridges agroecology and regenerative supply chain management by translating agroecology's practicebased evidence into supply chain design terms for agri-food systems. Drawing on an abductive systematic
literature review of 72 peer-reviewed articles, we synthesize four agroecology-informed supply chain practice
sets: agroecological production, knowledge transfer and co-creation, embedded and solidarity-based market
relationships, and organizing forms for value chain coordination. These practices were conceptualized into four
agroecological supply chain configurations—bridged, scaffolded, civic, and territorial—that differ in who coordinates exchange, how demand is structured, and where capabilities and governance reside. We further map
the four practice sets to core regenerative supply chain management principles and highlight distributive justice
as a cross-cutting condition for durable regenerative outcomes. The study elaborates a developmental logic,
which indicates that producer-led, territorially embedded chains supported by local innovation networks and
deep producer-consumer integration are positively associated with systemic regeneration.

1. Introduction
Agricultural supply chains sit at the center of today's socio-ecological
sustainability crisis. Industrialized sourcing and distribution have
contributed to land-use change, biodiversity loss, and rising greenhousegas emissions, while simultaneously extracting value from and shifting
operational risks onto upstream producers (Richardson et al., 2023;
Ripple et al., 2025; Salmi et al., 2023). For purchasing and supply
management, this is not only a "sustainability" issue. It is a supply-chain
design issue: buyers, intermediaries, and producer organizations shape
which production practices remain viable through specifications, procurement routines, intermediation, and the market channels they build
and legitimize.
In response, supply chain scholarship has begun to articulate
regenerative supply chain management (RSCM) as an agenda that goes
beyond harm reduction and asks how supply networks can regenerate
socio-ecological systems (Gualandris et al., 2024; Howard et al., 2019;

Pagell and Wilhelm, 2025; Wieland, 2021). Prior sustainable supply
chain research has often emphasized "do less harm" practices and incremental improvements, which can understate the scale of change
implied by ecological degradation (Pagell and Shevchenko, 2014; Salmi
et al., 2023). Yet, despite progress in conceptualizing RSCM, the literature still offers limited design-relevant synthesis for agri-food supply
chains. In particular, we lack a clear account of which coordination
arrangements and market linkages can support regenerative outcomes,
where capabilities need to reside, and how governance can move beyond
purely transactional exchange (Gualandris et al., 2024; Hsu et al., 2025).
Agroecology provides a long-standing evidence base on how food
production and exchange can be reorganized around ecological functioning and social equity (Wezel et al., 2009, 2020). Agroecology refers
to the application of ecological principles in agriculture (Wezel et al.,
2009), but it has evolved into a field that connects biophysical variables (e.g., soil health and biodiversity) with socio-economic and institutional dynamics (e.g., markets, policies, and cultural traditions)

2. Literature review
2.1. Regenerative supply chain management
Regenerative sustainability places ecological vitality at the center of
organizing and evaluates firms as embedded in socio-ecological systems
rather than separate from them (Dyllick and Muff, 2016; Fischer et al.,
2024; Hahn and Tampe, 2021). This paradigm rests upon adaptation
and recognizes that socio-ecological systems are inherently complex,
dynamic, and non-linear (Albareda and Branzei, 2024; Muñoz and
Branzei, 2021). For supply chains, this framing shifts the key question
from improving isolated practices to redesigning inter-organizational
relationships, governance, and performance metrics in ways that support the long-run viability of the places and communities that supply
networks depend on (Bag et al., 2025; Tanveer et al., 2025).
In line with this paradigm, RSCM asks how supply networks can
contribute to restoring or strengthening the socio-ecological systems on
which they rely (Gualandris et al., 2024, 2026; Pagell and Wilhelm,
2025) and calls on firms and scholars to disentangle from entrenched
extractive paradigms. The emerging RSCM literature emphasizes place
sensitivity, long time horizons, and adaptive governance in complex
systems (Albareda and Branzei, 2024; Muñoz and Branzei, 2021).
However, it remains less explicit about the concrete coordination and
governance arrangements through which regeneration is pursued in
agri-food supply chains, where ecological outcomes depend on
land-based production and multi-actor interdependence (Hsu et al.,
2025).
Across RSCM contributions, three mechanisms recur. First, supply
chains may require strategic reintermediation—new relational and
institutional arrangements that reconnect production with place and
enable coordination across actors (Gómez and Lee, 2023; Meyer et al.,
2026). Second, regeneration relies on capability portfolios that
combine tangible infrastructure with relational capacity for multi-actor
learning and governance (Bag et al., 2025; Tanveer et al., 2025). Third,
RSCM reorients performance measurement toward temporal and
regenerative metrics that track systemic viability rather than short-cycle
efficiency (Howard et al., 2019; Hsu et al., 2025). Consistent with

2.2. Agroecology as evidence base for regenerative supply chain design
The term agroecology was first conceptualized by Bensin (1925) to
describe the application of ecological principles in agriculture and link
them to the social and institutional conditions of production and exchange. It represents a comprehensive approach to designing participatory, bottom-up interventions aimed at transforming food systems to
be more equitable, resilient, and secure for all stakeholders (Gliessman,
2016; HLPE, 2019; Wezel et al., 2020). These interventions aim to
optimize interactions between plants, animals, humans, and the environment while simultaneously addressing the socio-political dimensions
of food systems—including dignity, equity, food sovereignty, and the
livelihoods of local territories (FAO, 2019; HLPE, 2019). For SCM, this
matters because agroecology specifies how diverse, low-input

3.3. Coding and synthesis
To analyze the papers, we designed a codebook based on the concepts from RSCM (e.g. synchronicity, proportionality, polyrhythmicity), stakeholder partnerships, and the elements of agroecology proposed by the Food and Agriculture Organization of United Nations (FAO, 2019; Gualandris et al., 2024; HLPE, 2019). In the first
coding round, we identified agroecology-related supply chain practices,
enabling or constraining factors, and stakeholder cooperative relationships. This round produced 135 first-order codes. We define "agroecological practices" as recurring, interdependent actions that coordinate
production and exchange in ways that support agrobiodiversity and
ecosystem services.
In the second coding round, we aggregated first-order codes into
second-order categories based on the position in the supply/value chain
(upstream, midstream, downstream). In the third round, we used
pattern matching to identify recurring combinations of second-order
categories and group the evidence into internally coherent agroecological supply chain configurations. We compared patterns along four
design dimensions that repeatedly appeared in the literature: (1) agroecological production practices (e.g., crop-rotation, crop-livestock
integration, regenerative agriculture), (2) knowledge transfer and cocreation (integral training versus farmer-led innovation networks), (3)
embedded and solidarity-based market relationships (e.g., mediated
procurement, community-supported agriculture, territory-product value
chains), and (4) organizing forms for value chain coordination (intermediary coordination versus cooperative coordination). We iterated
these groupings until configurations were mutually distinct and stable,
resolving disagreements through team discussion.
Finally, we extracted the socio-economic and environmental outcomes reported in the reviewed studies and mapped them to the practice
bundles and configurations to characterize their potential contributions
to socio-ecological regeneration.

3. Method
We conducted a systematic literature review (SLR) following Durach
et al. (2017) and Sauer and Seuring (2023). The review comprised six
stages and fourteen decisions (see Fig. 1).
3.1. Scope of the review
We defined the SLR scope in line with the research question and
focused on agroecological practices and organizing arrangements that
shape supply chain design in agri-food. We adopted an abductive
approach, iterating between the agroecology evidence base and
emerging RSCM concepts to develop supply-chain-relevant categories.
Because agroecology research is extensive and often agronomic, we
restricted the review to peer-reviewed journal articles in English that
connect agroecology/regeneration with inter-organizational coordination beyond on-farm performance. As many agroecology studies use the
term "value chain," we included both "value chain" and "supply chain"
terms in the search string.
We conducted our keyword search in Scopus, chosen for its
comprehensive coverage of peer-reviewed literature across management, environmental, and agricultural sciences (Mongeon and Paul-Hus,
2016). To ensure a robust search string, several researchers independently tested various queries, refining them through coordination
meetings until reaching a consensus. We applied two keyword sets to the
title, abstract, and keyword fields: the first comprised broad ecological
terms (e.g., agroecology, regeneration, socio-ecological systems), while
the second targeted specific supply chain functions (e.g., sourcing, value
chains, supply management). This approach captured 289 articles
examining the intersection of regenerative practices and supply chain
management. The full dataset is available in Online Supplement A.
To enrich the discussion and embed the findings from agroecology to
the SCM discipline, we conducted a targeted scoping of foundational and
recent conceptual work in sustainable and regenerative supply chain
management to develop a sensitizing vocabulary for theorizing. These
SCM sources were not included in the SLR search, screening, or coding
procedures and did not alter the review corpus.

3.4. Validity and reliability
Validity and reliability were ensured through the use of redundant,
parallel work at key milestones. These milestones included defining the
final query, the set of articles, the codebook, the conceptual model, and
the key results. The cloud-based version of the software ATLAS. TI
facilitated concurrent work and coordination, helping minimize inconsistencies and mitigate potential individual biases. Four researchers
coded the articles and two researchers discussed results. The two subteams cross checked work and exchanged feedback.

4.1. Typology of agroecological supply chains
As a result of the pattern-matching on how agroecological practices
unfolded from the farm up to the consumers, we conceptualized four
configurations of agroecological supply chains (see Table 3 and Fig. 3).
The configurations differ along four recurring design dimensions in the
reviewed literature. Below, we describe each configuration using illustrative examples reported in the reviewed studies and summarize reported outcomes and boundary conditions (Table 4).
Configuration A—Bridged supply chains rely on an external intermediary to structure demand and coordinate aggregation, matching
supply with buyers' requirements. The reviewed studies describe this
configuration in settings where farmer organizations lack consolidation
infrastructure or formal market access and where intermediaries provide
the coordination interface. Demand is commonly organized through
third-party enabled markets (e.g. school feeding programs or NGOenabled purchasing) (Guerra et al., 2017; Valencia et al., 2019). Reported outcomes include improvements in market access and
income-related indicators, but the studies also flag dependence on
external organizations and limited producer–consumer embeddedness
as boundary conditions (Barrios et al., 2020; Fonseca et al., 2025).
Configuration B—Scaffolded supply chains also rely on intermediaries to structure demand, but they differ in the intensity and
purpose of knowledge transfer. Here, intermediaries invest more
directly in strengthening farmers' organizational capabilities (e.g.,
governance, marketing, consolidation, and coordination routines)

4.3. Knowledge transfer and knowledge co-creation
Across the studies analyzed, we identified two sets of collaborative
practices that support the diffusion and implementation of agroecological production practices: knowledge transfer through integral training,
and knowledge co-creation through local networks of innovation. Integral training refers to collaborative programs or initiatives implemented
by intermediaries (e.g., public sector or NGOs), in which farmers receive
training in production, entrepreneurship, market access, finance, and
governance, with the aim of strengthening rural organizations' capabilities, and degree of agency (see Table 5).
There is high variation in how the training programs are implemented. The literature highlights the effectiveness of deployment
through farmers' networks for knowledge diffusion. This is a train-thetrainers approach, in which lead farmers receive formal training at a
local institution, and then they go back to their communities and
organize spaces for training other farmers (Sekiya et al., 2020; Wezel
et al., 2016). For instance, the Kilimanjaro Agricultural Training Center in Tanzania, where farmer leaders are trained and then return to
teach other farmers, setting up demonstration plots (Sekiya et al., 2020).
Local innovation networks are defined as farmer-led systems for
knowledge co-creation and polycentric governance (Hett et al., 2023).

4.4. Embedded and solidarity-based market relationships
To differentiate agroecological products, consumers must recognize
their nutritional benefits and positive socio-environmental impacts,
which conventional markets often fail to internalize (Bellon-Maurel
et al., 2022; Lanka et al., 2017). To prevent commoditization, these
products require socially embedded relationships that bridge the gap
8

J.A. Rodríguez et al.

Journal of Purchasing and Supply Management xxx (xxxx) xxx

between producer and consumer. We identify three primary direct
market linkages for this purpose: community-supported agriculture,
third-party enabled markets, and territory-product value chains (da
Silva et al., 2024; Levidow et al., 2021).
Community-supported agriculture is an alternative food network, a
direct-to-consumer model that builds a strong, solidarity-based
connection between local farmers and consumers (Albrecht and Wiek,
2021; Voge et al., 2023). This participatory structure serves as a primary mechanism for redesigning regenerative supply chains by shifting
governance power toward a collective agency, in which farmers and
consumers share the financial risks and rewards of farming. Customers
pay an upfront subscription fee to a local farm, this connection facilitates
a reduction in food loss and waste between 22% and 70% compared to
traditional systems (Voge et al., 2023). Moreover, community-supported
agriculture supports the economic viability of complex agroecological
systems, such as the Ökohof Waldgarten food forest, which successfully
distributes 120 diverse food boxes per week to 200 members, thereby
incentivizing the maintenance of multi-layered agrobiodiversity
(Albrecht and Wiek, 2021).
Third-party enabled markets refer to transactions between agroecological producers and buyers enabled by intermediaries, such as NGOs
or governmental organizations. These enabled markets create structured
demand that reduces producer vulnerability and operational risks. We
observed two types of enabled markets: public programs that source
food from farmers for meals in schools, and NGO programs that assist
farmers in capability development and connection with buying firms.
For instance, in Brazil, the Programa Nacional de Alimentação Escolar
(PNAE) supported nearly 1.2 million farmers all over the country,
benefiting about 45 million students in public schools. This program is
committed to purchase 30% of the food from smallholder, agroecological farmers (Valencia et al., 2019). Farm Africa, an NGO, promoted a
forest management approach, in which farmers adopt agroforestry
practices in bamboo forest; the program benefited over 3900 families
integrated into cooperatives with commercial forest use, participating
farmers doubled product unit prices (e.g., incense or bamboo stems),
reaching about 480,986 ha of forest (Farm Africa, 2025) (see Table 5).
A significant challenge in these enabled markets is the dependency
on the resources of the intermediaries. The literature emphasizes
decentralized support structures to maintain a local presence across
multiple territories (Hett et al., 2023; Sekiya et al., 2020). For instance,
Farm Africa utilizes a hub-and-spoke model, where headquarters oversee global strategy while local staff and farmer-to-farmer networks
manage daily operations (Farm Africa, 2026). However, such
geographic dispersion may dilute the deep cultural and value-driven
change associated with agroecology (Barrios et al., 2020; Guerra
et al., 2017).
Territory-product value chains refer to an entrepreneurial scheme in
which a product label is built upon the values of the territory and the
production methods (Lanka et al., 2017; Makino et al., 2020). Producers
exercise a leadership role through social proximity, establishing a territorial identity that re-embeds supply chains within local
socio-ecological relations (Levidow et al., 2021). This degree of agency
allows farmers to build collective capacities, democratic
self-organization, political support from civil society groups, and economic independence from waged labor (Levidow et al., 2021).
For instance, the Mountain Partnership Product label is a certification scheme granting a label of the products (e.g. food, beverage or
handicraft) that tells a story of the origin, the territory on which the
products come from, the techniques used in the production, the nutritional profile, or the significance of local culture (FAO, 2026). This
scheme is part of the Mountain Partnership, a United Nations alliance
dedicated to the advancement of sustainable mountain development, a
network of over 520 members, including 60 national governments, 18
intergovernmental organizations, and more than 300 civil society organizations (FAO, 2025). The Initiative empowers smallholder farmers
and overcomes the high logistical costs of difficult terrain (Fréchet,

2019). By integrating agroecological training, cooperative formation,
and direct market linkages, the program boosts productivity and negotiation leverage while bypassing intermediaries to increase profit margins. Additionally, the initiative focuses on value-added processing and
collaborative policy advocacy to ensure a supportive institutional
environment for mountain-specific farming standards.
In summary, solidarity-based markets link agroecological producers
with value-driven consumers to foster community well-being, sustainable livelihoods, and agrobiodiversity. These direct, local relationships
allow farmers to leverage transparent production methods and regional
distinctiveness as key differentiators. By providing alternative networks,
these models effectively prevent the commoditization of agroecological
products.
4.5. Organizing forms for value chain coordination
Agroecological production entails a higher variety of products, and
the commercialization of these products is more complex. Farmers are
connected to more than one supply chain, and they need additional
machinery or facilities to process the additional crops. For instance,
ranchers combining alfalfa and beef cattle need to invest in specialized
harvesting and drying equipment for maintaining cost efficiency and
quality control of the alfalfa (Moraine et al., 2016). Also, monocultures
such as maize often have high-volume, well-established trade routes,
whereas in rotated crops, legumes such as soybean or beans would
require different buyers, which farmers may not be aware of (Vanlauwe
et al., 2019). We observe in the literature that the coordination between
agroecological production and consumption is arranged through two
forms: intermediaries and well-established farmers' cooperatives.
As previously mentioned, intermediaries such as NGOs or public
sector, enable market linkages, and coordinate the consolidation of
products to match demand volumes. This approach is more common in
developing economies, in which farmers lack the consolidation infrastructure, the formalization or organizational forms to connect with the
market (Boissière et al., 2020; Glaser and Diele, 2004). In this regard, the
intermediaries aim to strengthen the organizational capabilities of
farmers' cooperatives so they can eventually undertake the consolidation, processing, and commercialization of their crops. The key challenge, though, is the autonomy of the cooperatives that enter such
programs. There is limited evidence on the autonomy of these organizations after the assistance program ends (Boissière et al., 2020; Sam and
Zabbey, 2018).
Additionally, well-established farmers' cooperatives play a pivotal
role in the coordination of agroecological production and consumption.
For instance, cooperatives make the crop-livestock integration more
efficient and economically viable, these organizational forms can
manage the consolidation, processing, quality control, provision of seeds
and equipment for integrating pasture or plant crops in cattle cooperatives; balancing long-term supply and demand, and having the
necessary scale for supporting the logistics and resource mobilization
such as biogas collection, financial services, and marketing function
(Levidow et al., 2021; Low et al., 2023; Moraine et al., 2016). Cooperatives are also well positioned to add value such as processing fruits
into jam or juices, and milk into cheese (Esquivel et al., 2021; Martin
et al., 2016). In participatory guarantee systems for organic certifications, cooperatives also coordinate the assurance of the standards,
reducing the cost for an effective quality control method for smallholder
farmers (Levidow et al., 2021). Hence, cooperative organizational forms
are deployed to coordinate agroecological production with demand.
4.6. The potential for regenerating socio-ecological systems
The four identified agroecological supply chain configurations
demonstrate significant potential for farm-level soil regeneration and
nutrient cycling. The potential for system regeneration refers to the
scale-out and scale-deep of agroecological practices by the actors of a
9

J.A. Rodríguez et al.

given socio-ecological system. Hence, a supply chain's capacity for systemic regeneration is a function of its territorial depth, geographical
scope, and the degree of embeddedness between producers and consumers. By contrasting this regenerative potential with coordinating
structures—specifically producer-led versus externally managed
chains—we identify a consistent empirical regularity: producer-led,
territorially embedded chains supported by local innovation networks
and deep consumer integration are associated with systemic regeneration (see Table 3). This pattern is illustrated in Fig. 4, which maps
regenerative potential (Y-axis) against geographical scope and
producer-consumer embeddedness (X-axis). The X-axis is bifurcated: the
area extending toward external coordination represents the scaling out
logic, while the area extending toward producer-led coordination represents the scaling deep logic.
Bridged supply chains are in the bottom-left of Fig. 4, it is an
intermediary-led configuration with a presence in several territories, but
with little knowledge transfer to producers. In this regard, the literature
indicates that when structured demand is coupled with training programs, the impact is deeper on livelihoods and the adoption of agroecological practices (Fonseca et al., 2025; Guerra et al., 2017).
Scaffolded chains integrate these mechanisms, yet they have limitations
in building deep connections between consumers and producers, and the
scaling out depends on the resources of the intermediary, and on its
decentralized model for supporting daily operations in dispersed territories. Then, civic chains combine structured demand, knowledge
co-creation, and deep relationships between consumers and producers
but at the cost of scaling out, this supply chain configuration is highly
territorial, with challenges for expanding to other territories. The
contrast between scaffolded and civic chains reflects an inherent tension
between scaling out and scaling deep in the process of designing
regenerative supply chains.
Finally, territorial supply chains, in the top of Fig. 4, combine farmerled knowledge co-creation, and place-based product labels to decommoditize agroecological products. In this configuration, some demand
structuring efforts remain, but agroecological products are already in
the market competing with a supply chain consisting of participatory
and regenerative process with local ecosystems. From the elaborated
configurations of chains, territorial have the highest potential for system
regeneration, this chain configuration must dynamically adapt to the
persisting tension of scaling deep and scaling out. Read as a heuristic
continuum, the typology suggests that the key design attributes for
regenerative supply chains are the knowledge co-creation with producers, the embeddedness of relationships with consumers, and the

coordination of supply chains by local actors.
In summary, increasing coordination capability of local actors and
embeddedness between farmers and consumers is positively associated
with the potential of system regeneration by supply chains. The patterns
underlying the typology also indicates that the process toward regenerative supply chain starts with external mediating organizations
developing production, and organizational capabilities of agroecological producers. The scaling out of agroecological production depends on
the capabilities of intermediaries to support farmer-to-farmer connections that enable the emergence of a local innovation network. Then, as
local organizations strengthen, they are oriented to developing close and
embedded relationships with consumers, while preserving the identity
and the conformance to agroecological production methods. Finally,
local organizations deploy participatory approaches and entrepreneurial
agreements to develop territory-based product labels to connect with
global markets and govern the supply chain.
5. Discussion
Taken together, the typology depicts four agroecology-informed
supply chain configurations that vary by coordination, market linkage,
and the locus of governance. These configurations do not form a rigid
sequence; rather, they can coexist within the same territory. Still, the
reviewed studies suggest a developmental logic that can increase
regenerative potential in some settings: as collective capabilities and
locally embedded governance deepen, coordination can shift from
externally led arrangements toward producer-led and territorially
grounded ones. We therefore use Fig. 4 as a heuristic continuum for
comparison, not as evidence of a universal path that cases always follow.
Our findings operationalize key principles proposed in the RSCM
literature (see Table 6). Agroecological production practices (e.g.,
agroforestry, crop–livestock integration) enact proportionality by
aligning input use and outputs with ecosystem functioning and by
leveraging economies of scope rather than scale. Embedded and
solidarity-based market linkages (e.g., community-supported agriculture and territory-product chains) enact reciprocity by stabilizing demand, sharing risk, and reshaping value distribution between farmers
and consumers. Farmer-led innovation and cooperative organizing
structures enable poly-rhythmicity by creating collective sensing,
learning, and coordination mechanisms that synchronize production
and logistics with seasonal and ecological rhythms. For purchasing and
SCM, these principles become actionable through supply chain design
choices in sourcing specifications, demand commitments, assurance
mechanisms, supplier development routines, and the governance of intermediaries and cooperatives. This mapping clarifies how agroecology
provides a practice-based "how-to" for RSCM's call to redesign supply
chains as ecocentric networks that regenerate socio-ecological systems
(Gualandris et al., 2024; Howard et al., 2019).
The configurations also speak to a central debate in RSCM about
intermediation. In bridged and scaffolded designs, intermediaries such
as NGOs, extension agents, or public procurement programs often
structure demand and provide coordination and capability-building
infrastructure that producer organizations cannot initially supply.
Civic and territorial designs show that regeneration often depends on
purposeful reintermediation: building or strengthening locally
embedded intermediaries (e.g., cooperatives, food hubs, participatory
guarantees, place-based labels) that re-embed exchange in territory,
knowledge co-creation, and shared accountability (Meyer et al., 2026).
This perspective aligns with SCM arguments that midstream actors can
shape food environments, coordinate collective action, and translate
ecological practices into credible market offerings, while also making
visible the governance conditions under which such intermediation
supports—rather than replaces—producer autonomy (Gómez and Lee,
2023).

supply chains viable and that are central to purchasing and supply
management. We identify three embedded linkage forms that stabilize
demand, share risk, and make production practices credible to buyers:
community-supported agriculture and similar short circuits, mediated
markets such as public or NGO-enabled procurement, and territoryproduct value chains supported by place-based narratives or labels
(León-Bravo et al., 2025; Pullman and Dillard, 2010; Williams et al.,
2023). These market linkages highlight how procurement rules, demand commitments, and assurance arrangements can either enable or
undermine regeneration in practice (Gualandris et al., 2024; Guthey
et al., 2014; Valencia et al., 2019). They can be interpreted as reintermediation mechanisms that redirect demand and rebuild relational
accountability after disintermediation, a core issue in RSCM (Gómez and
Lee, 2023; Meyer et al., 2026).

differentiate regenerative supply chain designs: integral training and
farmer-led innovation networks. These mechanisms connect agroecology evidence to SCM discussions of supplier development and capability
building, but they also show a distinct logic. In many agroecology settings, capability building resembles inclusive innovation, where farmers
co-create knowledge through joint experimentation with universities,
NGOs, and public agencies rather than receiving one-way capability
transfer against buyer-defined targets (Hoffecker and Lee, 2024; Miller
et al., 2022). This contribution links the scale-out/scale-deep tension
to supply chain design and to emerging RSCM studies showing that
regeneration requires a portfolio of tangible, intangible, and relational
resources. Training and decentralized support can extend reach, while
farmer-led networks can deepen territorial embeddedness and governance, often with different coordination costs and risks (Bag et al., 2025;
Tanveer et al., 2025).

chains (Carmagnac, 2021; Jordan et al., 2016; Rodríguez et al., 2016).

6. Conclusion
This study develops an agroecology-informed lens for regenerative
supply chain management by synthesizing evidence from 72 peerreviewed articles and translating it into supply chain design terms. We
identify
four
agroecology-informed
supply
chain
practice
sets—agroecological production, knowledge transfer and co-creation,
embedded and solidarity-based market relationships, and organizing
forms for value chain coordination—and show how they combine into
four recurring supply chain design configurations: bridged, scaffolded,
civic, and territorial. The configurations form a developmental logic,
which indicates that producer-led, territorially embedded chains supported by local innovation networks and deep producer-consumer
integration are associated with systemic regeneration.
This review also has limitations. As an abductive synthesis of published studies, it cannot establish causal effects or evaluate the net
impact of any configuration; it maps reported outcomes and mechanisms as they appear in the literature. The evidence base is uneven
across geographies, commodities, and outcome measures, and the
reviewed studies vary in how they operationalize regeneration, which
constrains comparability. Our search strategy may under-capture relevant SCM work that does not use agroecology or regeneration terminology, and we rely on authors' reporting of practices and outcomes
rather than standardized indicators. Future research should therefore
test the proposed configurations and their boundary conditions through
comparative and longitudinal designs, examine how procurement and
contracting mechanisms shape capability development and governance
across configurations, and develop feasible metrics for regenerative
performance and distributive justice at the supply chain level.
Although the evidence base reviewed here is specific to agri-food,
some of the coordination mechanisms identified—such as reintermediation, knowledge transfer and co-creation, embedded market relationships, and place-based product labels—may be analytically
transferable to other natural-resource-dependent supply chains.

5.2. Managerial and policy implications
Managers and policy makers can use the four configurations as a
diagnostic to choose sourcing, capability-building, market-linkage, and
governance mechanisms that fit their territorial conditions. In bridged
and scaffolded designs, buyers and downstream actors can reduce earlystage risk by partnering with credible intermediaries (e.g., NGOs,
extension agencies, or procurement programs) that structure demand
and support coordination. These partnerships work best when they
specify a handover plan and build organizational routines inside producer groups, including planning, quality control, aggregation, basic
logistics, and market-facing capabilities. Without that handover, mediated markets may improve access while leaving producers dependent on
external coordination capacity.
In civic designs, managers can align purchasing with diversity and
seasonality by using relational contracting and demand commitments
that share risk, such as subscriptions, forward commitments, and cropplanning agreements. These mechanisms help transform variability
into a design feature that supports low-waste fulfillment and diversified
production. In territorial designs, managers can invest in cooperative
coordination assets that raise value capture and make practices legible at
larger scale, including aggregation and scheduling, simple processing
and cold storage, participatory assurance systems, and governance for
place-based narratives or labels. Rather than rely on a single outlet,
managers can build a portfolio of embedded channels and match crop
diversity to segment needs across that portfolio.
Across configurations, useful candidate indicators include the farmer
share of final price, producer decision rights in key transactions, crop
diversity and seed access, delivery reliability, and food loss and waste.
These indicators help managers assess when external coordination remains necessary and when producer organizations can assume new
functions such as assurance governance, label stewardship, or negotiated local contracts.
Policy makers can act as market makers when local organizations
remain nascent. Public procurement, mediated standards, and decentralized extension services can create predictable demand and learning
networks that de-risk early adoption. These instruments are most
effective when implemented through decentralized territorial presence
and paired with investments designed for local governance and capability transfer. Small, well-placed investments in micro-infrastructure
(aggregation points, cold storage, shared processing), cooperative
development services, and low-cost assurance systems (e.g., participatory guarantees) can make regenerative practices visible without
excluding smallholders. In this sense, public programs can be designed
to strengthen producer value appropriation, expand producer decision
rights, support biodiversity stewardship, and reduce food waste while
maintaining accountability to place.
Finally, for managers and policy makers using an RSCM lens, the
configurations highlight the role of midstream regenerative
intermediaries—such as cooperatives, food hubs, and procurement
agencies—in coordinating learning, investing in place-based infrastructure, and aligning performance metrics with socio-ecological
regeneration and distributive justice objectives (Gómez and Lee, 2023;
Howard et al., 2019; Karaosman et al., 2025).
